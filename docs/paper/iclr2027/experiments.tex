\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\textbf{Datasets.} We evaluate on three standard image-classification
benchmarks. CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100~\citep{krizhevsky2009learning} are
consumer photographs of the kind the backbone saw in pre-training,
while EuroSAT~\citep{helber2019eurosat} is Sentinel-2 satellite imagery
and sits further from it. Embeddings come from the frozen DINOv2
ViT-B/14 encoder~\citep{oquab2023dinov2}, and
Appendix~\ref{app:backbones} repeats every comparison on CLIP
ViT-B~\citep{radford2021clip}, both of dimension $d = 768$. For each
dataset the unlabeled pool $\mathcal{U}$ holds 10,000 held-out images
of the same distribution and never overlaps the labeled sample.
\textbf{Configuration.} One configuration serves every dataset and both
encoders. Truncation keeps $d' = 128$ dimensions, whitening pools the within-group covariance over
$C = K$ $k$-means groups, and smoothing averages each embedding over
$\kappa = 10$ pool neighbors with weight exponent $\gamma = 3$. The
score is the prototype softmax of \eqref{eq:score}. Its temperature $T$
is the one quantity resolved per dataset and encoder, fixed from the
unlabeled pool before any label is seen and then held constant across
every trial and budget. Appendix~\ref{app:config} details the rule and
the sensitivity of every configuration choice.
\textbf{Baselines.} We compare against split CP on a trained linear
head, scored by the three standard conformal
scores~\citep{sadinle2019least, romano2020aps, angelopoulos2021raps},
SemiCP~\citep{zhou2026semicp}, which augments calibration with
nearest-neighbor-matched unlabeled scores, and
CV+~\citep{barber2021predictive}.
% probe+CP row pending g3 (R2): selftrain / pool-MLP reviewer arms.
Every split-based baseline divides its labels between training and
calibration. We grant each its most favorable allocation per cell,
selected with hindsight from $n_{\mathrm{tr}}/n \in \{0.25, 0.5,
0.75\}$, and its best score, so all comparisons favor the baselines.
SemiCP follows the official implementation (adaptations in
Appendix~\ref{app:baselines}).
The unlabeled pool is available to every method that can consume one.
\textbf{Protocol.} The labeled budget is indexed by shots per class
$s = n/K$, tabulated at $s \in \{2, 4, 6, 8\}$. Each cell
averages 50 independent trials that resample the labeled sample, the
test points (5 per class), and where applicable the baseline's
train/calibration split. Labeled samples are class-balanced.
Prediction sets follow the non-empty convention: a method whose
conformal set comes back empty predicts its top-ranked label instead.
Every method follows it, the reported size counts that label, the
coverage credits it, and the guarantee is untouched because sets only
grow.

We report $\alpha = 0.1$ in the main text and $\alpha = 0.05$ in
Appendix~\ref{app:alpha005}, with three metrics: marginal coverage,
mean prediction-set
size, and
the class-conditional
coverage gap
$\mathrm{CovGap} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_c |\mathrm{cov}_c - (1-\alpha)|$ in
percentage points, reported in Appendix~\ref{app:covgap}.

\textbf{Below the boundary, spending every label on calibration wins.}
At $s = 2$ split CP and SemiCP return 64 to 98 percent of the $K$
candidates on every dataset, while FRCP returns 1.0 to 3.6 labels and
the smallest set in all three cells. CV+ is the only competitive
alternative there, at 1.3 to 3.5 times the FRCP size and under the
weaker $1 - 2\alpha$ guarantee. Across the table FRCP is smallest in
every one of the 12 cells.
\textbf{Set sizes are comparable only because coverage is.} FRCP holds
marginal coverage at or above the target in every cell, between 0.901
and 0.984. The high end is CIFAR-10, where sets are essentially
singletons and the non-empty convention turns what used to be
abstentions into extra coverage. SemiCP falls below the target in two
cells, both on EuroSAT, reaching 0.885, so part of the size advantage
it shows there is bought back from coverage rather than earned on
efficiency.
\textbf{The advantage narrows as the budget grows.} On CIFAR-100 the
CV+ set is 3.4 times the FRCP set at $s = 2$ and 18 percent larger at
$s = 8$, and on EuroSAT the two methods meet at $s = 8$, 1.52 against
1.51. Neither shift reaches the ranking, and CV+ remains the strongest
baseline in every cell. The picture repeats on CLIP ViT-B
(Appendix~\ref{app:backbones}), where FRCP is smallest in 8 of 12
cells at both miscoverage levels and every loss is to CV+, three of
them on EuroSAT, whose crossover arrives within the grid on the weaker
encoder.

\textbf{Runtime.} Full conformal prediction does its work at test
time, and Table~\ref{tab:runtime-cifar100} measures exactly that
phase. The naive per-candidate sweep is what earned full CP its
reputation: 29 to 79 seconds to conformalize the 500-point CIFAR-100
batch, growing linearly with the calibration size. The closed-form
batched sweep of Appendix~\ref{app:cost} returns the identical
p-values, verified bit for bit, in 0.5 to 0.6 seconds across the whole
grid, a 60 to 130 times reduction. The inductive baselines threshold a
trained probe at test time and cost a few hundredths of a second, so
FRCP pays about half a second more per 500-point batch. In exchange
its preparation is the lightest of every method, 0.05 to 0.18 seconds
against 1.9 for CV+'s fold probes, on top of the one-off
pool-transform fit that every batch shares. On the two $K = 10$
datasets every method, naive sweep included, finishes within 0.16
seconds. Tractability, in short, is a property of the sweep
implementation, not of the construction.

\subsection{Refinement ablation}
\label{sec:ablation}
Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} ablates the refinement stage by stage on
every dataset under the deployed score: the raw embedding, each single
stage, the truncation-plus-whitening pair, and the full chain.
Appendix~\ref{app:ablation} tabulates the decomposition, and
Appendix~\ref{app:scorehist} shows what the surviving subset does to
the score distributions.
\begin{figure}[t]
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_stage_ablation_row.pdf}
\end{center}
\caption{Stage ablation. Mean prediction-set size for the raw
embedding, each single refinement stage, the T$+$W pair, and the full
chain, with truncation (T), whitening (W), and smoothing (S), at 2, 4,
and 8 labels per class and $\alpha = 0.1$ over 20 trials. The dot
matrix marks the active stages. Bars above a panel cap are clipped and
annotated with their value. Sizes are shown before the non-empty fill
of Section~\ref{sec:setup}, which only adds the empty-set mass.}
\label{fig:ablation}
\end{figure}
\textbf{Refinement pays most at small budgets, and only the full chain
delivers it.} The full pipeline returns the smallest sets in all nine
dataset and budget cells of Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}. On CIFAR-100 it
reduces set size by 79\% at $s = 2$ (10.7 $\to$ 2.27), by 36\% at
$s = 4$, and by 11\% at $s = 8$. No single stage gets below 4.16 there
at two shots. Truncation and whitening together deliver the largest
step, and smoothing removes up to a further third (3.35 $\to$ 2.27).

\textbf{Order matters, not just membership.} Whitening alone
destabilizes the temperature fit, which collapses to its floor on every
dataset, and its sets inflate past the raw embedding on EuroSAT at all
three budgets (6.40 against 5.51 at $s = 2$). With truncation first the
fit is stable and the pair improves on either stage alone on CIFAR-10
and CIFAR-100. EuroSAT only breaks even at the pair, and there
smoothing lands the decisive step (4.32 $\to$ 3.65 at $s = 2$), which
is the ordering Section~\ref{sec:refine} fixes.

\textbf{Refinement separates the score mass around the quantile.}
Appendix~\ref{app:scorehist} plots the true-class and false-class
distributions of the deployed prototype-softmax score before and after
refinement. Refinement concentrates the true-class mass below the
calibration quantile and pushes false-class mass above it, so the same
coverage is bought with fewer false labels in the set: at four labels
per class the leak falls from 0.55 to 0.02 on CIFAR-10 and from 1.50
to 0.42 on CIFAR-100. Even on EuroSAT, where the two masses stay
entangled, the leak drops from 1.84 to 1.56.

\textbf{Smoothing the representation, not the score.}
Appendix~\ref{app:snaps} compares the two levels at which a pool
correction can act (Table~\ref{tab:snaps-vs-smoothing}): the
representation-level smoothing stage recovers more of the refinement
gain than a score-level SNAPS correction at the small budgets that
matter, at comparable per-trial cost, and stacking the two adds little.
